# Build a Chronicle Society case from my topic

**How to use this file.** Start a new Claude chat, upload or paste this whole file, and then say what you want a case about. Give it as much or as little as you like — a unit plan, a set of readings, a chapter of a novel, a science topic, a list of learning outcomes, or just a sentence. Claude will ask a few questions and then build you a complete investigation your class can run.

Everything below this line is written for Claude.

---

## What you are making

The Chronicle Society is a fictional bureau that investigates unsolved cases. A **case** is a paper-and-screen mystery in which students are given a folder of evidence about a real topic and have to work out what happened — not by guessing, but by **eliminating every explanation the evidence will not support**.

The structure is fixed, and it is the structure that does the teaching:

1. **Read everything.** Seven-ish documents land on the desk. No shortcuts.
2. **A four-digit lock.** Four questions, four single-digit answers, each from a *different* document. This is a comprehension check that feels like a puzzle.
3. **More evidence is released.** Four more documents.
4. **A keyword lock.** A word built by doing something — a crossword, a cipher, a register, an acrostic. This is the analysis step.
5. **The elimination board.** Five explanations. For each one, students pin the *single* piece of evidence that makes it impossible.
6. **The accusation.** They choose a conclusion and write their reasoning, naming at least two pieces of evidence.
7. **The debrief.** Four questions that are not answered for them.

The pedagogy is the point: students cannot finish without reading closely, cross-referencing sources, distinguishing a fact from a conclusion, and justifying a position in writing. That is why it works as a test of a unit — the case cannot be solved by anyone who did not learn the material.

## Step 1 — Interview the teacher before you build anything

Ask these, in one go, before writing a word of the case. Use a multiple-choice question tool if you have one; otherwise ask plainly and wait.

1. **What is the topic?** And what material should the case be built from — a unit plan, readings, a text, or your own knowledge of the subject?
2. **What should students be able to do by the end of the unit?** List the two or three outcomes. The locks and the board will be built to test exactly these.
3. **What year level, and how long?** Default: Years 7–8, 60–100 minutes, teams of two or three.
4. **Is the subject matter real or invented?** This changes the rules — see Step 2.
5. **Paper, screen, or both?** Paper is the default deliverable and works with no devices.

If the teacher has given you documents, read all of them before asking anything. If they have given you a bare topic, say what you plan to build in three sentences and check before writing it out.

## Step 2 — The truthfulness rule

This matters more than anything else in this file.

The case format is persuasive. It produces official-looking documents, witness statements and reports. Students will believe them. So:

**If the topic is real** — a historical event, a real person, a real place, real science:

- **Never invent a quotation and attribute it to a real named person.** If you need a voice, use an unnamed role ("the harbourmaster", "a mill worker") and mark it as a reconstruction.
- **Every document carries a source line at the foot** — the actual source the facts came from, exactly as the Bible cases cite chapter and verse.
- **Every reconstructed document says so.** A short italic line: *"Reconstruction. The figures are from the 1953 survey; the wording is not."*
- **Every factual claim a student could repeat in an exam must be checkable.** If you are not confident of a number, do not put a number in.
- The teacher notes must contain a section headed **"What is real and what is reconstructed"**, listing both. This is not optional.

**If the topic is invented** — a fictional scenario built to test a science or maths unit, or a case set inside a novel — say so plainly on the cover, and you are free to write it however you like.

**If the topic is a text the class is reading** — treat the text as the primary source. Quote it accurately and cite by chapter and page. Invent only the investigator's apparatus around it.

When in doubt, invent the *scenario* and keep the *content* true. A fictional stream in a real catchment, tested with real methods, teaches the unit and lies to nobody.

## Step 3 — Design the case

Work these out before writing any documents, and show the teacher this summary for approval.

**The premise.** Two or three sentences. Something is wrong, missing, or unexplained. The best premises have a fact that should not be possible: five ambushes and nobody came; a sealed tomb and no body; a stream with clean water and no fish.

**The five explanations.** Each must be *genuinely plausible* — a reader should be able to believe any of them at the start. For each one, name the **single document** that makes it impossible. Not "evidence that argues against it": the one fact that kills it.

This is the hardest part of the design and the most important. Test each pairing by asking: *could a clever student defend this explanation after reading that document?* If yes, the document is not decisive enough.

**The answer.** The sixth option — the one left standing. It should be the explanation that survives elimination rather than the one that sounds most sensible at the start.

**The documents.** Around eleven to fifteen, in three groups:

- **Open file (6–8)** — what the team starts with. Include the briefing letter, the central record or dataset, two or three witness or expert statements, and a map, plan or diagram.
- **Bundle B (3–4)** — released by the first lock. The technical evidence: the measurements, the analysis, the thing that was tested.
- **Bundle C (3–4)** — released by the second lock. The resolution: what was actually going on, and what happened afterwards.

**Lock 1 — four digits.** Four questions, each answered from a *different* document, each answer a **single digit**. Where the material allows, **make one of the answers zero** — "no samples exceeded the limit", "nobody was told", "not one returned". Students assume a zero question is broken, and the moment they realise it is not is the moment the case turns.

Write the questions so they test the unit's recall outcomes. This is your comprehension assessment.

**Lock 2 — a keyword** that is written nowhere in the file. Pick a mechanic:

- **Crossword** — 12 clues, all answerable from the documents; six marked squares spell the keyword. Best for vocabulary-heavy units.
- **Register or table** — a number column tells you which letter to take from each row's name. Best for data units.
- **Acrostic** — first letters of the answers to a set of questions, in order.
- **Cipher** — a Caesar shift with the key given on a decoder card. Best when you want a quick win.

Make the keyword *meaningful* — the word that names the answer, so assembling it is itself the discovery.

**The debrief.** Four questions, none of them answered. At least one should be about the method rather than the content: what made this hard to work out, which evidence was hardest to explain away, where a real investigator could have gone wrong.

## Step 4 — How the parts map onto assessment

Tell the teacher this, because it is why the format is worth their time:

| Part of the case | What it tests |
|---|---|
| Lock 1 — four questions from four documents | Recall and close reading. Cannot be answered without having read. |
| Reading the open file | Sustained reading for detail across multiple sources. |
| Lock 2 — the keyword mechanic | Applying a rule, vocabulary, or working with data. |
| The elimination board | Analysis. Telling a decisive fact from a supporting opinion. |
| The accusation and reasoning | Written justification. This is the markable piece. |
| The debrief questions | Reflection and transfer. |

The **case record sheet** — the one-page write-up students hand in — is where you see the reasoning rather than the answer. Make sure your case produces one.

## Step 5 — Write it

### The voice

This is what makes a case readable rather than a worksheet in costume.

- **Documents are voices, not summaries.** People deny things, get defensive, volunteer awkward facts, and say more than they mean to. A witness statement should sound like somebody talking.
- **Every document is worth reading on its own.** If a page exists only to pad the folder, cut it.
- **One handwritten margin note per document at most** — the investigator nudging, never explaining.
- **Short sentences carry the weight.** "He told nobody. Not that day, not after."
- **Never tell the reader what to conclude.** Lay the fact down and move on.
- **The obvious suspect is a real suspect.** Give them motive and let the evidence clear them.

### The document format

Each document has: a **kind** label (Witness statement, Field report, Ledger, Plan), a **title**, a one-line **subtitle**, the **body**, and a **source line** at the foot.

Useful shapes: a briefing letter from the case supervisor; a table or register; a witness or expert statement in the first person; a technical report with findings; a timeline or day log; a map or plan with labelled features; a follow-up file describing what happened afterwards.

### The images

If the teacher wants illustrations, generate them elsewhere and paste them in. The house style, which keeps everything looking like one archive:

> **STYLE:** Hand-drawn antique illustration on aged cream parchment, in the manner of a 19th-century archaeological field plate. Sepia, ochre, tan and faded ink-brown palette with muted olive-green accents. Fine pen cross-hatching and light watercolour wash. Visible paper grain, foxing spots, softly worn deckled edges. No people. Wide landscape format, 3:2.

For a modern or scientific topic, swap that for:

> **STYLE:** A clean technical plate on off-white paper, in the manner of a government survey report. Restrained palette — slate grey, ink blue, muted ochre. Fine line work, measured and unfussy. Subtle paper texture. No people. Wide landscape format, 3:2.

Then add the subject and, if the picture needs labels:

> Add N blank rectangular label plaques with thin dark borders and empty interiors, each connected by a fine leader line with a small dot: one to X, one to Y. **Absolutely no text, letters, numbers or writing anywhere in the image.**

Always ask for blank plaques and add the labels yourself afterwards. Image models cannot spell reliably at small sizes.

## Step 6 — What to deliver

Produce these in order. Check the design with the teacher after the first one.

**1. The design summary** — premise, the five explanations with their decisive documents, the answer, both lock codes, and how it maps onto their outcomes. Half a page. Get approval before writing the documents.

**2. The paper edition** — the printable pack. This is the main deliverable and it works with no devices:

- Cover with the case title and a cut-out label for the front of a folder
- A teacher preparation page: what to print, what to cut, what goes in which envelope
- **The investigator's manuscript** — a numbered walkthrough the team runs itself from
- Every document as a cut-out card with a dashed border
- **Envelope labels** — eight of them. Two carry the real bundles and are labelled with the correct code and keyword; six carry a slip reading *"Not this one — your working is wrong somewhere."* This is how the locks work on paper: the team's answer tells them which envelope to open.
- The elimination board: five explanations with glue-down slots, plus a page of evidence tags to cut out
- The accusation form with tick boxes and ruled lines
- A one-page case record sheet for them to hand in
- A certificate
- **The answer key**, marked *TEACHER — REMOVE THIS PAGE*, containing the codes, the board pairings, the conclusion, the full eliminations, prompts for stuck groups, and the "what is real and what is reconstructed" section

## A worked example — how a science unit becomes a case

To show the pattern on a non-narrative topic. A Year 7–8 unit on freshwater ecosystems and water quality.

**Premise.** A stream that has been surveyed by the same school every year for a decade. This year the water tests clean on every measure the students know how to run — and there is almost nothing living in it.

**The five explanations, and what kills each:**

| Explanation | Killed by |
|---|---|
| Drought — the stream ran too low | The flow records: this year's flow is the third highest in ten years |
| Fertiliser run-off from the farm upstream | The nitrate results: within range at every site, including directly below the farm drain |
| The dairy factory discharge | The discharge consent and the sampling below the outfall: nothing detectable, and the factory shut for six weeks of the sampling period |
| A disease among the insects | The survey: it is not one group missing, it is everything above a certain point in the stream |
| Nothing is wrong; the survey method changed | The method sheet, signed, identical to last year's |

**The answer.** A new culvert was laid under the farm track in autumn. It is perfectly clean, perfectly legal, and its lip sits 40 cm above the water — so nothing can get past it going upstream. The water is fine. The stream is cut in half.

Notice what that does. The chemistry is a red herring students have to disprove *properly*, and the answer requires the concept the unit is actually about: a habitat is not just water quality, it is connectivity.

**Lock 1 — 3708.** How many sites were surveyed? → 3 · How many years of records exist? → 7 (a decade of the programme, seven with this method) · How many nitrate readings exceeded the guideline? → **0** · How many weeks was the factory shut? → 8.

That zero is the case in miniature. Every team assumes the question is broken.

**Lock 2 — CULVERT**, built from the site register: each site's name plus its number in the "barriers recorded" column gives one letter.

**The board** pins flow records, nitrate results, the discharge consent, the invertebrate survey and the method sheet against the five explanations.

**Debrief.** *"The water was clean. Why was that the most misleading fact in the file?"*

Two other shapes, briefly. **A novel study:** the case is a genuine question the text leaves open — who knew what, and when — with the chapters themselves as the evidence and page references as the source lines. **A history unit:** an event with competing accounts, where the documents are the real primary sources and the eliminations are about what each source could and could not have known.

## Before you hand it over — the checklist

- [ ] Every explanation is genuinely plausible at the start.
- [ ] Every explanation is killed by **one** decisive document, not by a general weight of argument.
- [ ] Each of the four lock-1 answers comes from a different document, and one of them is zero if the material allows.
- [ ] The keyword is not written anywhere in the file.
- [ ] Every document is worth reading. Nothing is padding.
- [ ] Every document has a source line, and every reconstruction says it is one.
- [ ] No invented quotation is attributed to a real named person.
- [ ] The teacher notes say what is real and what is reconstructed.
- [ ] The answer key is on its own page and clearly marked for removal.
- [ ] The debrief asks four questions and answers none of them.
- [ ] The case cannot be solved by a student who did not read the material. If it can, the questions are too easy.
